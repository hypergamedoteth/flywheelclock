How Disney makes money

The wheel, redrawn

Acquired opens the Walt episode with this line: "The Walt Disney Company is the most successful enterprise ever created for monetizing human nostalgia." The mechanism is older than that sentence. In early 1958 the Wall Street Journal ran "Disney's Land: Walt's Profit Formula: Dream, Diversify, and Never Miss an Angle." A studio artist drew the circular diagram for the article. Walt did not. The hosts say Disney's archives and the Journal both confirmed that. The word flywheel is not in the piece. The chart below is a new drawing of the same idea. 3

Six eras, and the break

The story as Acquired tells it. Near-death marks are the years the hosts say the company almost stopped. Dollars from before FY2025 are theirs, unless a filing is cited. 34

  1. 1901–1966Walt
    Near death · Oswald, 1928Rebirth · 1928, 1937, 1955

    Walter Elias Disney is born in December 1901. The studio that lasts starts October 16, 1923, when the Alice Comedies get a contract, $1,500 to $1,800 a short, and Roy is the business. 3

    Early 1928, Universal owns Oswald and Mintz walks off with most of the animators. The hosts' lesson, burned in from then on: own the IP. Mickey follows. Steamboat Willie premieres November 18, 1928. 3

    Snow White premieres December 21, 1937. The hosts' figures: about $1.5 million to make, about $8 million in rentals, the highest-grossing film to that point. Then a war, a strike, and thin years. 3

    Disneyland opens July 17, 1955. The company lists on the NYSE in November 1957. The diagram is the next year's Journal story. Walt dies December 15, 1966, of lung cancer, at 65, in the middle of the EPCOT pitch. Ben Gilbert's stat: 99.95% of the market value, set against the 1966 company, was created after Walt died. 3

  2. 1966–1984Wilderness
    Near death · 1984

    Roy O. Disney finishes the Florida park. The Magic Kingdom opens in 1971. He dies about two and a half months later. The city-sized EPCOT Walt wanted does not get built. 3

    Animation thins out. The hosts count three features from 1971 to 1984. In 1983 the stock falls from $82 to $52. In 1984, they say, parks and consumer products made a quarter-billion dollars of profit and film and TV made $2 million. Worth more in pieces than as a company. The Bass brothers come in at about 25%. 4

  3. 1984–1994Eisner, Wells, Katzenberg
    Rebirth · the musicals

    September 7, 1984: the board pushes out Ron Miller. Within 14 days, Michael Eisner and Frank Wells are hired. Jeffrey Katzenberg comes from Paramount to run the studios. Roy E. Disney chairs animation. Parking at the parks, the hosts say, was still a dollar. 4

    The features return. Home video, the stores, Broadway, and the resort build-out are the new spokes. The hosts say operating profit went from well under $300 million in 1984 to just under $2 billion a decade later. 4

    Frank Wells dies in a helicopter crash in 1994. That is the end of the trio. 4

  4. 1994–2005The break
    Near death · Save Disney, 2004ESPN comes in, 1995

    Katzenberg leaves, sues, and the hosts say the suit later settled for a reported $280 million. He starts DreamWorks and hires away animation talent. 4

    1995: Capital Cities/ABC for $19 billion. ESPN is the piece that compounds. The hosts cite about $9.42 a month per subscriber from cable operators, and they say the cable-networks segment was 60% of company operating income in 2008–2011. They add that analysts believed about three quarters of that segment was ESPN. Not a FY2025 figure. 4

    Michael Ovitz lasts just over a year as president and leaves in December 1996. The hosts put the severance at $140 million. 4

    February 2004: Comcast bids $54 billion in stock, the morning of investor day. March 3, 2004: 43% of shares withhold support for Eisner. Bob Iger is the internal candidate. He gets the job in 2005, on three ideas the hosts repeat: branded films, technology, and the world outside the U.S. 4

  5. 2005–2020Iger
    Rebirth · buy the libraries

    The buying: Pixar in 2006 for $7.4 billion of stock (the hosts also net out about $1 billion of cash, to about $6.4 billion), Marvel in 2009 for $4 billion, Lucasfilm in 2012 for $4 billion. On the Walt episode the hosts say Oswald comes back in 2006 in a trade that sent Al Michaels to NBC. 345

    The hosts' decade, 2005 to 2015: operating income from $4.7 billion to $14.7 billion, revenue from $32 billion to $52 billion. Their figures, not re-derived here from old filings. 4

    August 2016: a minority stake in BAMTech. The 2019 episode puts that stake at $1 billion and the whole outlay at $2.6 billion. Disney+ launches in November 2019. Fox's entertainment assets close in 2019 at $71.3 billion, bid up from a $52 billion deal announced in December 2017. 45

  6. 2020–2022Chapek
    Shock · COVID

    Bob Chapek is CEO from 2020 to 2022. The hosts put COVID, streaming losses, and the parks' exposure to travel shocks in this stretch. They name 9/11 and COVID as the two big ones. Iger returns in 2022. This page does not add a Chapek playbook the episodes don't give. 4

  7. 2022–Iger again
    FY2025 · parks at a record

    The filing is the scorecard. Experiences operating income $9,995 million, which the earnings release calls a record. Direct-to-consumer operating income $1,327 million, from $143 million in FY2024. Linear networks revenue down 12%. Sports revenue flat, operating income up 20%. 12

    About 231,000 employees at year end. Disney+ at 131.6 million paid subscribers. Attendance headcount still unpublished. 1

The playbook

What the hosts treat as the operating rules, plus the case for and against the stock of businesses. This is not investment advice. 34

  1. 1
    Own the IP

    Oswald is the scar. Universal owned the rabbit, so the studio owned nothing when the distributor walked. The hosts say the company never forgot. 3

  2. 2
    Keep the main telling scarce

    Animation compounds, on their argument: the characters do not age out and do not have to be paid like stars. The saying Iger used, as they tell it: "As animation goes, so goes the company." 35

  3. 3
    Make the other lines earn

    Roy's 1958 line, above, is the rule. A film is also a park day, a shirt, a comic, a tape, a musical. Secondary stuff can be everywhere. The primary film stays the scarce one. 3

  4. 4
    Refill the center

    When Disney Animation thinned, Iger's answer was to buy whole libraries: Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, then Fox. The hosts' test is whether the new stories can feed every spoke, not whether one opening weekend works. 4

  5. 5
    Own the pipes

    BAMTech, then Disney+ and Hulu, so the customer is not rented to Netflix or a cable bundle forever. The hosts say a near-deal for Twitter died, and that owning rails is a different bet from owning a social network. 45

  6. 6
    ESPN is a second machine

    Affiliate fees carried the company for years. They are not the 1958 wheel. Cord-cutting is why Sports can be flat on $17.7 billion of revenue and still be the structural worry. 41

The hosts' bull case

  • Parks nobody else has. FY2025 experiences operating income was $9,995 million. 1
  • Direct-to-consumer is now $24,614 million of revenue, and it made money: $1,327 million, up from $143 million. 1
  • Marvel, on their telling, refilled the center of the wheel for a generation. 5

The hosts' bear case

  • Linear revenue fell 12% in FY2025. Sports affiliate and subscription fees fell 1%. The cable toll is shrinking. 1
  • Fox at $71.3 billion is the buy they regret. Empire first, return second. 4
  • Parks stop when travel stops. They name 9/11 and COVID. The 10-K still does not say how many people walked in. 41
  • Too many sequels, and the question of who runs it next. Franchise fatigue and succession are their risks, not a number in the filing. 4

Questions

How does Disney make money?

In the year ended September 27, 2025, Disney reported $94.425 billion of revenue. Entertainment was $42.466 billion, Sports $17.672 billion, and Experiences $36.156 billion, after $1.869 billion of intercompany eliminations. Experiences produced $9.995 billion of segment operating income, more than Entertainment ($4.674 billion) and Sports ($2.882 billion) combined. 1

What is Disney's flywheel?

Acquired's account of a 1958 Wall Street Journal diagram: make films people love, then let television, merchandise, music, and the park reinforce them. A studio artist drew that diagram. Walt did not. The FY2025 version of the shape adds streaming and sports, and the profit sits in the parks. 31

Who drew Disney's 1957 synergy chart?

A Disney studio artist, for a Wall Street Journal article in early 1958 titled Disney's Land: Walt's Profit Formula. Acquired's hosts say Disney's archives and the Journal confirmed it was not a napkin sketch by Walt. This page does not reproduce the drawing. 3

How much do Disney's parks make?

Experiences, which includes parks, resorts, cruises, and consumer products, reported $36.156 billion of revenue and $9.995 billion of operating income in FY2025. Domestic and international parks and experiences, excluding consumer products, were $31.711 billion of revenue and $7.817 billion of operating income. Those two sums add the supplemental lines in the 10-K. Disney does not publish absolute park attendance. 1

What did Disney pay for Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, and Fox?

As cited on Acquired: Pixar $7.4 billion in 2006, Marvel $4 billion in 2009, Lucasfilm $4 billion in 2012, and 21st Century Fox's entertainment assets $71.3 billion, closed in 2019 after a $52 billion announcement. Cap Cities/ABC, including ESPN, was $19 billion in 1995. BAMTech was about $2.6 billion. These are the hosts' figures, not lines in the FY2025 10-K. 45
